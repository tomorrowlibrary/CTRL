## So what is algorithmic trading?

I guess I must bow to my own stupidity. I led with a passionate, inspirational letter that assumed you knew what algorithmic trading was, and then turned around and started describing it anyway. Anyhow, here it is once again, properly this time.

Start with the words. *Algorithmic* comes from al-Khwarizmi, which is literally the name of the good sir, a ninth-century Persian mathematician in Baghdad, whose book on calculating with Hindu-Arabic numerals was so popular that medieval Europe made a word out of his name. And *trading* comes from trading. Duh.

No, the actual English word comes from Middle Dutch and Low German, where *trade* meant a track or a path. It shares a root with *tread* and with the Danish *træde*, to step. Being Danish, that is my closest understanding of it: your stepping path, the path you walk.

So, in a similar way, I wish to take you down this trading path.

Divide the words and it is that simple: algorithmic trading is trading, with mathematics, systems and algorithms added to enhance an already profitable kind of business. And I am nearly entirely sure I will not miss my shot if I propose this: you, dear reader, came because you are interested in mathematics, you might just wish you could study for your entire life, and you would also like to take a holiday every once in a while. So CERN is on the back burner and Two Sigma is at the front. (What a fuffing wonderful, mathy name that company has. And Bluefin Capital? Who the fuf made that name, a bunch of sea urchins?)

So, long story longer: algorithmic trading is the business of trading goods, assets and so on with scientifically optimised systems. It is like taking the engineers and scientists of CERN and pointing them at asset prices rather than particle streams. (Now imagine the reverse, a bunch of bankers building a particle accelerator. That is a theatre show I would want live-streamed to my home TV. And on a side note, if you want a TV show about life on a trading floor, look up *Industry*.)

That, in essence, is the *algorithmic* in algorithmic trading. Underline that word for this part. What my fellow STEM people lose in all this is the other word. We understand a great deal about guiding particle streams, controlling flows and measuring quantum effects. But I guarantee you that the majority of us come out of academia knowing just about squat about trading.

### So what the fuf is trading?

We have all been on Instagram late one night, scrolling past an ad from some finance bruva showing off his 15th Jaguar (vroom, vroom), his 3rd blow-up doll and the two cardboard boxes he calls his mansion. On a more serious note, a lot of them rent that shit for the videos so they can rip us off. If you did not know this, it is a real thing that you can research and study. I did for a moment. It was fun.

On Instagram, my fellow finance bruvas tell you to invest in something. You do. You buy. The price rises 10, 20, 30 or even 100% in an hour or a few days. Then Mister Influencer dumps, and the price goes to −10, −20, −30 or −100%. This is a simple trading scam called a pump and dump.

Take the scam away, though, and trading is simply this bruva's idea: buy low, sell high. In essence. Or, in reverse, sell high and buy back low. (That is a short: you borrow the thing, sell it, and buy it back cheaper to return it.)

In trading our main goal is profit. Wow! Give this man a Nobel Prize! Yeah, anyhow. Our main goal is profit, but like the central question of economics, "how are things valued?", that can have so many different answers that it could be a study in itself. Philosophy, perhaps.

- Profit can be made by matching buyers and sellers. That is **market making**.
- Profit can be made by knowing the correct price when the market has it wrong. That is **relative value trading**.
- Profit can be made by being the fastest to buy at a wrong, lower price and sell at the higher, "corrected" one. That is **latency arbitrage**. High-frequency trading, essentially.
- Profit can be made by knowing the right relationships between different assets. That is **statistical arbitrage**: pairs trading, cross-exchange trading and so on.
- Profit can be made by knowing long-term patterns and trader behaviour. That is **trend following** and its cousins.

Our goal is therefore to be able to measure, whatever the idea, whether it is profitable.

### What the flying fuf do you mean, 2 + 2 is not 4?

Trading, unlike anything we meet in our physics, mathematics, computer science or engineering courses, is a business subject. It is much more akin to accounting or supermarket management than to particle physics at CERN or developing a new suspension for cars. But science is being used better and better in every aspect of our lives, and trading, business management and sales have all benefited massively from the methods we learn in our STEM degrees.

What we must shake off is the textbook, academic outlook many of us arrive with. Trading, like everything in business and in the real world outside the lovely, sheltered life of academia, is ABSOLUTE FUCKING CHAOS. The politeness of "fuf" must be dropped here, because reality out there is a war zone.

Where my course instructors and professors told me that 2 + 2 is always 4, you will find that this is in fact only true some of the time. Why? Because in the markets, fair value depends on who else is in the market. An asset is worth whatever the next counterparty will pay, and that depends on whether anyone shows up, how many show up and how well informed they are.

That is why we talk about trading edge and predictive edge as two different things. Those who find the right patterns are not necessarily the ones who can take advantage of them. Market making, for example, does not rely on correctly predicting the next move. It simply relies on posting the right bids and asks. Trend following does depend on predicting when a trend starts and ends, and even a correct prediction does not have to end in profit.

So we are not necessarily looking for the best description of the market. We do not need to capture its complexity, and whether your model is interesting or not does not really matter. All we want is profit in the real markets.

> Trading is the act of standing between counterparties whose valuations disagree, and capturing part of the gap.

Which means we do not even need to be right. We only need to be less wrong than the bloke on the other side of the trade, by more than it costs us to trade. In trading that advantage is called **edge**, and we want a better edge than the next person.

## The parts of algorithmic trading

> So, without further ado, here are the secrets of the universe as we know it...

Algorithmic trading goes beyond simply stating "edge is buy low, sell high". It is about demonstrating, statistically, that a certain behaviour, dynamic or idea is real and gives you an advantage over the markets (read: every idiot under the sun trading with or against you).

To get there, the work is usually divided along the trading pipeline: data intake, alpha and research, risk, inventory and portfolio management, and finally order management and execution. Each part serves a different purpose in the overall plan of "profitability", and each has its own section of this guide. Read the short version here, then branch off wherever you want to go deeper.

### 2. Alpha

Alphas are the ideas we implement: a view on a certain dynamic, behaviour or phenomenon, and how we decide to exploit it. An alpha is simply an idea that has edge. In rough terms, alpha comes in three flavours.

- **Liquidity provision.** We offer to buy from those who want to sell and to sell to those who want to buy, at times and in ways that are profitable to us.
- **Market inefficiencies.** Sometimes the market is simply filled with idiots. A buyer posts "buy at 102". A moment later, or on another venue, a seller posts "sell at 100". The correct price is 101. So we buy from the seller at 100, sell to the buyer at 102, and gain 2. We corrected the price and got paid for it.
- **Market inevitabilities.** In some sense this is the most controversial one to mention. With or without anyone's consent, the markets will swing. So buying low and selling high is inevitable for somebody, and to be that somebody you must understand relative prices: low and high compared with what?

**Branch off →** [Market dynamics and alpha](https://tomorrowlibrary.github.io/CTRL/market-dynamics/index.html) · [Known alphas](https://tomorrowlibrary.github.io/CTRL/alphas/index.html) · [Research studies](https://tomorrowlibrary.github.io/CTRL/research/index.html)

### 3. Risk

Risk is important because it does two jobs: it helps you correct yourself, and it keeps you from losing money unnecessarily. When we find good ideas, we want to reduce the risk of adverse events. Which will happen! Regardless of what your professors have been trying to teach you.

**Branch off →** [Inventory, portfolio and risk](https://tomorrowlibrary.github.io/CTRL/inventory-risk/index.html)

### 4. Inventory

Every trade leaves you holding something. Inventory is the question of what you hold, how much of it and for how long: first one asset at a time, then all of them together as a portfolio.

**Branch off →** [Inventory, portfolio and risk](https://tomorrowlibrary.github.io/CTRL/inventory-risk/index.html)

### 5. Execution

Order management and execution is where the idea finally meets the order book: which orders to send, at what price, in what size, when and where. Every order leaves a footprint and every trade costs something, so a good signal can still turn into a bad trade on its way to the fill.

**Branch off →** [Execution, orders and backtests](https://tomorrowlibrary.github.io/CTRL/execution-backtesting/index.html)

## No storytelling, please!

*I was recently told by my partner that I am a fucking storyteller, and that sometimes we just need a bloody summary rather than a story. So here is a summary for my fellow ADHD'ers.*

- We are here to <u>trade</u>. Nothing more, nothing less.
- But we really like certainty, not big returns on one-off event predictions.
- So we think mathematics and science can help make things more CERTAIN.
- But do not delude yourself that beautiful, complex mathematics wins here. Your one-billion-parameter model may be no better than a coin toss, or may give you one percentage point of predictive edge.
- We only want <u>trading edge</u>, and sometimes being right 60% of the time is worse than being right 40% of the time.

How do you measure trading edge?

$$
\text{Edge} \approx \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\text{exit price} \mid \text{future counterparties}] - \text{entry price}}_{\text{the gap you capture}} - \underbrace{\text{impact}(q, \text{depth})}_{\text{your own footprint}} - \text{costs}
$$

Or, in other words:

$$
\text{Edge} \approx \mathbb{E}[\,\text{net return} \mid \text{what you know now}\,]
$$

This, in essence, is the generalisation of "buy low, sell high", of "sell high, buy back low", and of "the prices on these currency pairs do not match, so buy one and hedge with the other". You get the idea by now.

- Once again: first and foremost we are TRADERS. Then we are mathematicians, computer scientists and physicists.
- Alphas are simply ideas that have edge. When we find good ideas, we want to reduce the risk of adverse events. Which will happen! Regardless of what your professors have been trying to teach you.
- The world is ugly and chaotic. Sometimes 2 − 2 = 4 and sometimes 2 + 2 = −3.
- IMC is kind when it lets you have some alphas for free, right off the bat. In reality, winning 55% of the time is fucking great. And that is after adjustments, parameter calibration, months of research, adjustable stop-loss and take-profit rules, selecting specific assets, venues and times of trading, accounting for costs, paying subscription fees up front to lower your trading fees, and paying on top for extra data.
- "Just do market making" or "just do buy and hold", with a near-100% win rate right off the bat, will make you feel really good about yourself.
- Having both the order book (the LOB) and the trade logs essentially hands you the success right off the bat.
- All you need to do now is propose valid trading ideas. They can start out as scientific hypotheses, but they must become trading ideas. Or search through [the massive pile of alphas](https://tomorrowlibrary.github.io/CTRL/alphas/index.html) in this guide for one that fits, and then improve on it.
- After that you must also look into inventory and execution, but I would guesstimate that alpha and a baseline implementation take you 75% of the way.

One final note: do not use AIs for alpha hunting. Beautiful and useful as they are, they have literally no f-ing clue. Use them instead to speed up EVERYTHING else. Let them help you test your hypothesis, then take the results, evaluate and improve.

If you simply try to jam in "I WANT MEEEAN REVERSION, WHY NO MEAN REVERSION?!!???!?", you will likely get an answer like "The ADF test and the Hurst exponent invalidate your hypothesis". Look at the scores, though, and they may well be close to passing, but far enough off for the hypothesis to be clearly invalid. This is due to the chaotic nature of the markets. Accept that defeat and test further: which idiot ideas would survive the chaos and the noise?

AI will only do EXACTLY what you ask of it. It cannot think on its own, no matter how much OpenAI and Anthropic want it to.

## Go and test it

Reading about trading only takes you so far. Here is where you can test your ideas, work on things and compete.

### IMC Prosperity: what the fuf is it?

Like the sea voyagers of ancient times before us, we now reach the sandy beaches of "What the fuf is this?", and this time discover that it is IMC Prosperity 5. Prosperity 3 was beachy, sandy and islandy. Prosperity 4 was spacey and starry, like landing on a planet. Who knows what adventure awaits us in Prosperity 5 and beyond.

The simple answer: it is an algorithmic trading competition, and likely the second most elaborate one available.[^1] The better answer, one you will have to think hard about, is that it is not a mathematics or science competition. It is a business case competition. I know the meaning and depth of that controversy is hard to take in, because you are most likely a maths, computer science, mechanical engineering or physics major and not a "business studies" kind of person.

Mathematics plays a large part, and it helps you refine certain processes. But the core skill we are looking for is a blend, and this is in fact a competition in TRADING. That must be made abundantly clear, and hopefully by the end it will be ingrained in your soul.

> We are simply doing trading.

The practical part: Prosperity runs once a year and mixes algorithmic rounds, where your Python bot does the trading, with manual ones. You can enter alone or in a team of up to five. It is built for university students, but anyone can sign up to learn. The last edition, Prosperity 4, ran from 14 to 30 April 2026. Sign up at [prosperity.imc.com](https://prosperity.imc.com/).

[^1]: Trading an FTMO free-trial account is likely the closest you will get without actually running an account in the markets. Yes, you can run algo setups there too, and get your ass handed to you so massively that you feel like you were run down by a train.

### More places to test your ideas

- **[WorldQuant International Quant Championship](https://www.worldquant.com/brain/iqc/).** A team competition in which you build alphas on WorldQuant's BRAIN simulation platform. It runs once a year: the 2026 edition opened in March and finished with finals in Singapore in October.
- **[Numerai](https://numer.ai/).** An ongoing tournament. You train machine learning models on obfuscated data to predict the stock market, and a hedge fund trades on the combined predictions of everyone taking part.
- **[Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions).** Trading firms such as Jane Street and Optiver have run forecasting competitions here on real, anonymised market data. The finished ones stay online with their data and the winners' write-ups, which makes them good practice.
- **Wunder Fund challenges.** Machine learning competitions on market data from a high-frequency trading fund, the RNN Challenge and Alpha Connectome among them. In the RNN Challenge you predicted the next state of a market from the states before it.
- **University team competitions.** If your university sends a team, look at the [UChicago Trading Competition](https://tradingcompetition.uchicago.edu) (algorithmic cases in market making, options and time series) and the [Rotman International Trading Competition](https://ritc.rotman.utoronto.ca) in Toronto (three days of simulated markets, every February).
- **[Optiver Ready Trader Go](https://readytradergo.optiver.com/).** A historical one. It no longer seems to run, but the earlier editions and the public solutions are still good practice in market making.
- **[FTMO Free Trial](https://ftmo.com/en/ftmo-free-trial/).** Not a competition, but the footnote above: fourteen days on a demo account under a prop firm's rules, free of charge. The real FTMO Challenge is a paid product. The trial is enough to meet the train.

More, including Citadel Securities Terminal and the CUATS Challenge, in the Library's [competition directory](https://tomorrowlibrary.github.io/competitions.html).
